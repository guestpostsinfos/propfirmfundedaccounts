The prop-firm industry's 2024–2026 shakeout — roughly 80–100 firms gone, some owing traders real money — wasn't random. The firms that collapsed shared a recognisable profile, and the warning signs were visible before the doors closed. Here are the seven that matter, and how to read them together.

## 1. Economics too good to be sustainable

The deepest red flag is an offer that doesn't add up: steep discounts *and* instant, uncapped payouts *and* enormous splits, all at once. Someone funds that math, and at a thinly-capitalised firm it's the next cohort of challenge buyers — a structure that works right up until sign-ups slow. If you can't explain how a firm stays solvent while paying everyone generously, assume it can't. Understanding [how prop firms actually make money](/guides/how-do-prop-firms-make-money/) is the fastest way to smell an impossible model.

## 2. Vague or frequently-changing rules

Ambiguity is a feature, not an accident. Rules loose enough to interpret at payout time are what let a struggling firm deny withdrawals selectively — "you violated the spirit of the consistency policy" is only possible when the policy was never precise. Firms that quietly reword their terms every few weeks are managing risk by moving the goalposts. Clear, stable, specific rules are a solvency signal in themselves.

## 3. A cluster of recent withdrawal complaints

Reviews are noisy, but *specific, recent, withdrawal-focused* complaints cut through. One angry trader is anecdote; a spike of detailed "requested three weeks ago, still pending, support went quiet" reports in the last month is a pattern. Weight recent complaints far above an aggregate star rating, because a collapsing firm's average is propped up by months of older happy reviews and paid affiliates.

## 4. No verifiable payout history

This was *the* differentiator in the shakeout. Firms with multi-year, documented payout records mostly survived; firms that couldn't show they'd paid consistently through a full market cycle mostly didn't. A firm founded last quarter isn't automatically bad — but it is automatically *unproven*, and unproven is a risk you're taking with your challenge fee. Watch, too, for a firm whose payout *timing* keeps slipping — unexplained delays are an early symptom, as [how long payouts take](/guides/how-long-do-prop-firm-payouts-take/) explains. This is why [our methodology](/methodology/) weights payout reliability at 35%.

## 5. Opaque ownership and jurisdiction

Who runs the firm, and where? Legitimate firms are increasingly transparent about their team and entity as regulators (CFTC, FCA, ASIC) move toward registration requirements. Anonymous ownership, a shell in a flag-of-convenience jurisdiction, and no named, accountable people are how operators keep the option to disappear. If nobody's name is attached, nobody's reputation is at stake.

## 6. Marketing that outweighs substance

When a firm's presence is 90% affiliate discount codes and influencer hype and 10% actual information about rules and payouts, that ratio tells you where the money goes. Heavy affiliate-driven acquisition isn't disqualifying alone — most firms use affiliates — but when it's the *entire* engine and the substance is thin, the firm is optimising for sign-up volume, which loops straight back to red flag #1.

## 7. Reliance on new sign-ups to fund old payouts

The others converge here. Every collapse pattern reduces to the same mechanic: withdrawals paid from incoming challenge fees rather than from a genuinely edge-positive book or real capital. You can't see a firm's ledger, but the *symptoms* are all above — impossible economics, shifting rules to slow payouts, complaint spikes, no history. When several appear together, this is almost always what's underneath.

## How to use this list

Don't hunt for a single smoking gun — **count the flags.** One can have an innocent explanation; three or more is a firm to avoid regardless of the discount.
